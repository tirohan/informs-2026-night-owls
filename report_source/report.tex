\begin{abstract}\small\noindent
The 2026 challenge asks for hourly Outage Severity Index (OSI) forecasts at four horizons for 63 counties whose outage record is withheld after March 13 23:00, using 239 fully observed counties for training. The four horizon columns are shifted views of a single 144-hour trajectory issued from one fixed origin, and we forecast that trajectory directly with two views of the same causal information. The first is a stack of two gradient-boosting members that see the same 206 features but learn different targets: a \emph{kinetics} member that predicts the residual from a first-order restoration prior, $\osi_{\mathrm{cut}}\,2^{-\ell/H}$, whose half-life $H\approx13$\,h is estimated from the training counties, and a \emph{direct} member that predicts the OSI level, combined with convex weights that move from the kinetics member (0.71 in forecast hours 1--12) to the direct member (0.89 beyond hour 48). The second is a small bidirectional selective state-space (Mamba-type) sequence model that reads each county's 216-hour weather record and its 72 observed outage hours as one sequence. In county-grouped, nested cross-validation the stack attains pooled RMSE of $0.01116/0.01017/0.00824/0.00748$ at $t{+}1/6/24/48$\,h and the sequence model alone ties it, but their fixed equal-weight average is 2--5\% better than either at every horizon (3--5\% better than the stack, paired-bootstrap improvement fractions 0.95--1.00), at $0.01078/0.00976/0.00801/0.00712$, 50/47/30/24\% below the hourly climatology of the training counties. We document where the remaining error sits---one county holds 29\% of it---and why the second storm wave is harder than the first. The pipeline is deterministic; the stack runs in minutes and the sequence model in about 70 minutes on one CPU thread.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem structure}\label{sec:problem}
The data package provides 216 hourly rows (March 11--19, 2026) for 302 counties in IN, OH, PA and WV, with 23 weather fields for every hour (2.5-km URMA analyses \citep{depondeca2011} for wind and temperature, ERA5 reanalysis \citep{hersbach2020} for the rest) and the outage state ($P_t$, $N_t$, $D_t$, $R_t$, counts, lags) for the first 72 hours only in the 63 test counties. Two wind events crossed the region (Fig.~\ref{fig:event}): the first peaks on March 13--14, when 131 of the 239 training counties reach their maximum OSI (110 on March 13 alone), and a second, broader event on March 15--17 that is comparable in wind but much weaker in outages (mean training OSI 0.020 on March 14 versus 0.005 on March 16).

Three structural facts drive our design. \textbf{(i) One origin, not 144.} The template asks for $\osi_{t+h}$ for every hour $t$ of March 14--19, but no outage information becomes available after the cutoff, so every entry is a forecast from the same information set and the four columns are shifted copies of one trajectory $S_c(j)$, $j=0,\dots,143$, where the forecast hour $j$ counts hours after March 14 00:00 ($j+1$ hours after the cutoff): the $t{+}h$ column at origin $j$ equals $S_c(j{+}h)$. Consequently the four RMSEs score \emph{nested windows} of the same trajectory (bottom of Fig.~\ref{fig:event}); the $t{+}48$\,h column only scores hours from March 16 00:00 onward and never sees the first-wave restoration. Predicting one trajectory per county and filling the columns by shifting it guarantees cross-horizon consistency and never imputes the withheld window, which the rules forbid. \textbf{(ii) Scored lead times range from $h{+}1$ to 144 hours after the cutoff.} No new observed outages can update a forecast after that origin. This does not preclude time-series models: SARIMAX can use future weather as exogenous inputs, as in the 2025 challenge study \citep{ye2025sarimax}, but a per-county model fitted to a test county's 72 observed hours must estimate that county's weather response from a single storm onset. We instead pool counties to learn weather-dependent trajectories (we do not evaluate SARIMAX here or claim that it cannot represent a second wave). Persistence of the cutoff value is a weak baseline (RMSE 0.063--0.069, worse than predicting zero) because so many counties sit near their peak at the cutoff: the task is a cross-sectional regression of trajectories on cutoff state and known weather. \textbf{(iii) The metric is dominated by a few counties and by the first day.} OSI targets are zero-inflated (42\% of scored $t{+}1$\,h cells are exactly zero; median 0.0001, maximum 0.545) and, because the loss is pooled squared error, ten counties carry 65--75\% of the target energy $\sum y^2$ and March 14 alone carries 78\% of it in the $t{+}1$\,h window. A model is therefore judged mostly on how well it renders first-wave restoration in severely hit counties---a different regime from the hurricane-outage literature, which predicts the \emph{onset} of damage from forecast winds \citep{guikema2014,fatima2024}.

\section{Data handling and causal feature construction}\label{sec:data}
\paragraph{Validation.} Both files are checked for unique county-hour keys, complete 216-hour grids, disjoint county sets and, in the training file, equality of every stored target with the shifted OSI. OSI is reconstructed in the observed window as $\osi_t=\max(0,\,0.40P_t+0.35N_t+0.25D_t-0.10R_t)$ (the test file carries no \texttt{osi} or \texttt{osi\_lag} columns); on the training file this reproduces the stored values to $5\times10^{-5}$.

\paragraph{Information boundary.} For every county, the outage record is sliced to timestamps $\le$ March 13 23:00 \emph{before} any outage-derived quantity is computed; the centred-smoothed $N_t$ and $R_t$ at the cutoff hour are organiser-supplied values that the methodology document explicitly permits. Weather is used at any hour, past or future, as the rules allow. The training file also contains three undocumented per-county constants computed over the whole event (\texttt{peak\_pct}, \texttt{peak\_customers}, \texttt{time\_to\_restore\_h}), plus \texttt{severity\_tier}; none is a feature, and \texttt{severity\_tier} and state only stratify cross-validation folds. A mutation test, run on every execution, overwrites every post-cutoff outage field and every whole-event column with sentinel values and asserts that the feature tensor is bitwise unchanged.

\paragraph{Features.} Each county yields a panel of $144\times206$ features indexed by lead $\ell=1,\dots,144$ hours after the cutoff, in five families: (a) \emph{cutoff state and history}---levels, lags (0--48\,h), window means/maxima/standard deviations and changes of $P_t$ and reconstructed OSI over 3--72\,h, means of $N_t$, $R_t$, $D_t$, net inflow $N_t-R_t$ at the cutoff, hours since the observed peak; (b) \emph{exposure}---$\log(1+\text{median customers tracked})$; (c) \emph{lead coordinate}---$\ell$, $\log(1+\ell)$ and the cutoff OSI decayed with half-lives of 6/12/24/48\,h; (d) \emph{weather at the target hour}---all fields with wind direction encoded as $(\sin,\cos)$, temperatures in $^\circ$C and dew-point depression; (e) \emph{weather context}---trailing means (3--48\,h) and maxima of gust and wind speed, lagged gusts, forward gust maxima over the next 1--24\,h, cumulative gust excess above 30/40/50\,mph since the cutoff, gust$\times$soil-moisture and gust$\times$cutoff outage fraction interactions \citep{cerrai2019}, and observed- versus future-window summaries. Identifiers, names, timestamps and states are never features.

\paragraph{Shape diagnostics.} We measured properties of the target and of its relation to the drivers on the training counties only (Table~\ref{tab:shape}; \texttt{shape\_diagnostics.py} reproduces every number). Each row fixes one design decision in Section~\ref{sec:method}: the zeros are not a separate onset process, the cutoff state loses its predictive value between 24 and 48 hours, and the wind response is strongly convex.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}
\subsection{A restoration-kinetics prior}
Restoration after a wind event is well described in the outage literature as a decay process whose rate depends on damage extent and crew capacity \citep{liu2007,nateghi2011}. Fig.~\ref{fig:kinetics}a shows the post-cutoff OSI of the 74 training counties with $\osi_{\mathrm{cut}}>0.02$, normalised by the cutoff value: after a short plateau most counties decay roughly exponentially, with a median $R^2$ of 0.86 for a one-parameter fit over the first 48 leads and per-county half-lives concentrated between 8 and 18\,h (Fig.~\ref{fig:kinetics}b). We therefore define a prior trajectory
\begin{equation}
\hat S^{\,0}_c(\ell)=\osi_{c,\mathrm{cut}}\;2^{-\ell/H},\qquad H=\operatorname{median}_c\hat H_c,
\end{equation}
where $\hat H_c$ is the least-squares half-life of county $c$ over its first 48 leads and the median is taken over the training counties of the current fit (it is re-estimated inside every cross-validation fold; the five outer fits give 12.8--13.2\,h, the 15 inner fits 12.3--13.7\,h, and all 239 counties 13.1\,h). The prior alone already removes 40\% of the climatology error at $t{+}1$\,h (Table~\ref{tab:results}). A 24-h half-life, the natural default, is too slow: the mean normalised OSI is 0.18 (median 0.17) after 24 hours, not 0.50.

\subsection{Two gradient-boosting members with one loss}
Both members are LightGBM regressors \citep{ke2017} trained on the pooled county$\times$lead rows of the training counties (191 counties $\times$ 143 leads $\approx$ 27{,}000 rows per outer fold) with the same 206 features and squared-error loss; they differ in target and capacity.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Kinetics member} predicts the residual $S_c(\ell)-\hat S^{\,0}_c(\ell)$; its forecast is $\max(0,\hat S^{\,0}_c(\ell)+\text{residual})$. It is deliberately small (350 trees, learning rate 0.035, 15 leaves, depth 5, minimum 100 rows per leaf, 85\% column sampling, $L_2=10$): the prior carries the decay and the trees learn only departures from it---delayed onset, faster or slower restoration, new damage.
\item \textbf{Direct member} predicts $S_c(\ell)$ itself with more capacity (800 trees, learning rate 0.02, 31 leaves, depth 5, minimum 60 rows per leaf, 80\% row and 50\% column subsampling, $L_2=10$). The decay curves are among its features and it spends 56\% of its split gain on them (Fig.~\ref{fig:importance}b): it reconstructs by splitting what the kinetics member is given in closed form.
\end{itemize}
This is a \emph{direct} multi-step strategy \citep{bentaieb2012,taieb2016}---one model for all leads with $\ell$ as an input---rather than a recursive one, the right choice when no new observations arrive to correct a recursion.

\paragraph{Horizon-balanced loss.} The official score is the mean rank over four pooled RMSEs, each computed on cells $j\ge h$. With one shared trajectory, the average of the four horizon MSEs equals $\sum_j w_j\,e_j^2$ per county with
\begin{equation}
w_j=\tfrac14\sum_{h\in\{1,6,24,48\}}\frac{\mathbf 1[j\ge h]}{144-h},
\end{equation}
so we train both members with these exact per-lead sample weights ($w_0=0$; lead 0 is never scored). They favour late leads slightly, because each late hour is scored four times; an unweighted fit is 0.4--4.1\% worse at every horizon (Table~\ref{tab:results}).

\subsection{Lead-dependent convex stacking}
The two members are complementary in time: the kinetics member is better while restoration dominates, the direct member once weather-driven damage dominates (Fig.~\ref{fig:skill}a). We combine them with non-negative weights that sum to one---Breiman's stacked regressions \citep{breiman1996}---fitted separately for three buckets of the forecast hour, $j\in[1,12]$, $[13,48]$ and $[49,143]$ ($j+1$ hours after the cutoff), by minimising the mean over horizons of the relative RMSE, $\tfrac14\sum_h \mathrm{RMSE}_h(\text{blend})/\mathrm{RMSE}_h(\text{climatology})$, plus a small quadratic pull ($0.01$) toward equal weights. The relative scaling stops the largest-error horizon from dictating the weights; the pull limits their variation across folds. Fitted on all development out-of-fold predictions, the weights are 0.71/0.29, 0.44/0.56 and 0.11/0.89 (kinetics/direct) in the three buckets---a monotone hand-over from restoration to damage.

\subsection{A sequence-model view of the same information}\label{sec:seq}
The stack sees the record only through summaries we chose in advance. To learn what those summaries miss, we trained a model that reads the raw record: each county is one 216-step sequence with 36 channels---the 25 weather channels at every hour; $P_t$, $N_t$, $D_t$, $R_t$ and reconstructed OSI, set to zero after the cutoff, with an observed-hour flag; hour-of-day sine and cosine; hours since the cutoff; log customers; and the prior of Eq.~(1) over the forecast hours---and the output is the trajectory read at the 144 forecast positions. The network is a two-layer bidirectional selective state-space model: each layer applies a Mamba (S6) block forward and one backward in time \citep{gu2023mamba}, so a forecast hour is informed by the observed record on its left and the known weather on its right through a state updated with input-dependent decay and input gates, the mechanism by which structured state-space models carry long-range dependence without attention \citep{gu2022s4} and the basis of recent Mamba forecasters \citep{wang2025mamba}. It is deliberately small (width 24, state size 8, 22{,}201 parameters, plain PyTorch with an exact chunked scan), trained with the same horizon-balanced loss, AdamW with a cosine schedule, early stopping on a 15\% county split of the training counties, and two seeds averaged. Reading in both directions is legitimate because the sequence contains no post-cutoff outage information; the mutation test of Section~\ref{sec:data} is applied to the sequence inputs too. The submitted trajectory is the \emph{equal-weight average} of the stack and the sequence model: cross-fitted convex weights on the development folds were 0.47--0.58, and a fixed half adds no estimated parameter.

\subsection{Validation protocol}
Because test counties are unseen, validation holds out whole counties: five folds stratified by state$\times$\texttt{severity\_tier}, all 216 rows of a county in one fold, as recommended for grouped and spatio-temporal data \citep{bergmeir2012,roberts2017}; each held-out county is represented exactly as a test county (72 observed hours plus weather). Stacking weights are fitted only on \emph{inner} out-of-fold predictions (three inner county folds within each outer training set) and applied to the untouched outer fold, so the reported stack scores are not contaminated by weight fitting. RMSEs are pooled over all held-out county-hours before the square root, as in the official metric. Uncertainty uses a county-level bootstrap (2{,}000 resamples); model comparisons use a paired county bootstrap of the RMSE difference (3{,}000), far more powerful than marginal intervals because the same severe counties dominate every model's error. Base seed 42 is combined with deterministic fold-specific offsets documented in the code; LightGBM runs in deterministic mode and PyTorch single-threaded with deterministic algorithms.

Table~\ref{tab:results} gives the ablation ladder. Relative to climatology the boosting stack reduces RMSE by 48.5, 44.6, 27.6 and 20.4\% at the four horizons (paired-bootstrap 95\% intervals for the difference exclude zero at every horizon). Across the five outer folds the half-life (12.8--13.2\,h) and the first-bucket kinetics weight (0.79--0.95) are stable; the later-bucket weights vary more (0.49--0.92 and 0.00--0.52), which the shrinkage toward equal weights is there to absorb.

Three comparisons carry the argument for the stack. \emph{Prior versus climatology}: a one-parameter prior with no weather information removes 40\% of the climatology error at $t{+}1$\,h. \emph{Residual versus level target}: with identical features and the same small configuration, learning the residual from the prior beats the direct level target by 9.2\% at $t{+}1$\,h and 3.4\% at $t{+}6$\,h (under 1\% at 24--48\,h), and the 13-h estimated half-life beats a 24-h default by 2.8--4.5\% at every horizon. \emph{Stacking}: the lead-bucketed stack improves on the kinetics member at every horizon (paired-bootstrap improvement fraction $\ge0.99$; mean gains $2.0$--$2.3\times10^{-4}$) and on the direct member at $t{+}1$ and $t{+}6$\,h (fractions 0.93 and 0.80), while at $t{+}24$ and $t{+}48$\,h it is within $0.6$--$1.0\%$ of the direct member, a difference the paired bootstrap cannot resolve ($95\%$ intervals $[-0.05,0.15]$ and $[-0.01,0.18]\times10^{-3}$); we keep the stack for its lower early-horizon error; the later-horizon intervals do not establish equivalence.

\paragraph{Two views of one information set.} The sequence model alone scores 0.01137/0.01006/0.00833/0.00727: a tie with the stack, better at 6 and 48\,h and worse at 1 and 24\,h, with paired-bootstrap improvement fractions of 0.43/0.65/0.33/0.79 and intervals that include zero at every horizon. Its errors correlate with the stack's at 0.83 across all scored cells---both fail on the same severe counties---and the gain from averaging is what that correlation implies: two estimates of equal error variance and correlation $\rho$ have an average with MSE $(1+\rho)/2$ of either, a 4\% RMSE reduction at $\rho=0.83$. The fixed equal-weight average is better than the stack by 3.3/4.1/2.7/4.8\% at $t{+}1/6/24/48$\,h (improvement fractions 0.95/1.00/0.98/1.00; mean gains $2.2$--$4.2\times10^{-4}$, 95\% intervals excluding zero at 6, 24 and 48\,h) and than the sequence model by 5.2/3.0/3.8/2.1\%. The gain is consistent: the average has lower RMSE than the stack in all five outer folds at 1, 24 and 48\,h and in four of five at 6\,h, and relative to climatology it removes 50.2/46.9/29.6/24.2\% of the RMSE. The county bootstrap gives 95\% intervals of $[0.0078,0.0141]$, $[0.0076,0.0119]$, $[0.0064,0.0097]$ and $[0.0050,0.0093]$ for its four RMSEs; the width reflects the dominance of a handful of counties (the bootstrap resamples fixed out-of-fold predictions, not refits). By forecast hour (Fig.~\ref{fig:skill}a) the gain over the stack is 3.1\% in hours 1--12, 2.0\% in 13--48 and 5.0\% beyond, and it is largest in the second wave (5.5\% on March 16--17), where the sequence model alone is already 3.5\% better than the stack: the trees see weather history through summaries fixed in advance and are piecewise constant in them, whereas the state-space model integrates the raw hourly record with learned time constants. This is the textbook gain from combining learners with different inductive biases on the same information \citep{breiman1996,makridakis2022}.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{Where the error is.} In the $t{+}1$\,h window, March 14 holds 78\% of the target energy and 54\% of the submitted model's squared error; March 16--17 (the second wave) holds 11\% of the energy but 25\% of the error. Ten counties account for 62\% of the squared error; Forest County, PA (FIPS 42053) alone for 29\%. Its OSI was 0.09 and still rising at the cutoff (net inflow $N_t-R_t>0$) and peaked at 0.545 six hours later; the model never predicts more than 0.13 (Fig.~\ref{fig:cases}, left). Ten of the 74 substantially affected training counties keep rising after the cutoff; six have positive net inflow $N_t-R_t$ at the cutoff, against one in five of the other affected counties, so a signal exists---but ten cases are too few for either learner to learn its magnitude, and squared error shrinks extreme predictions toward typical outcomes: over all cells with observed OSI above 0.05 the model predicts 78\% of the observed value on average. Under RMSE this shrinkage is the right trade-off for the population, but it is the model's largest identifiable weakness. Morrow County, OH (centre) is the typical case, a deep first-wave outage restored almost exactly along the prior; Clay County, WV (right) is the second wave, where the model anticipates the rise and its timing from the weather but under-predicts the peak by half.

\begin{figure}[tb]\centering\includegraphics[width=.66\linewidth]{fig4_cases}
\caption{Held-out trajectories for three training counties (out-of-fold predictions). Grey: the record the models were allowed to see; black: the withheld truth; dashed: the restoration prior; dotted blue: the boosting stack; purple: the submitted average.}\label{fig:cases}\end{figure}

\paragraph{Why the second wave is harder.} Across training counties, with wave 1 taken as March 13--15 and wave 2 as March 16--19 (\texttt{shape\_diagnostics.py}), the peak gust of the first wave explains outage peaks reasonably well (Pearson $r=0.41$ between peak gust and peak OSI), but for the second wave the same correlation is $-0.08$, and second-wave peaks are only weakly related to first-wave peaks ($r=0.15$). Two mechanisms are consistent with this: vegetation and equipment that fail in the first event are no longer there to fail in the second, and crews and mutual-assistance resources are already positioned \citep{guikema2014,wanik2015}. The data cannot separate the two; what a model can exploit is the interaction between the fragility revealed by the first wave and the second-wave winds, which is why weather features carry 59\% of the kinetics member's split gain and observed outage history plus customer exposure a further 28\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:importance}a). The resulting second-wave skill is real but moderate: predicted and observed second-wave county peaks correlate at 0.60 (Fig.~\ref{fig:skill}c) and the submitted model's March 16--17 RMSE is 27\% below climatology (the stack's alone: 23\%).

\caption{Top-12 features by share of split gain, averaged over the five outer folds. Once the decay is removed (a), the residual is explained mainly by weather history and the observed outage state; the direct member (b) spends most of its gain reconstructing the decay.}\label{fig:importance}\end{figure}

\paragraph{What did not help.} \texttt{experiments.py} re-runs the development alternatives on the same folds. A two-stage hurdle model \citep{cragg1971}---a LightGBM classifier for $\osi>0$ times a regressor on positive cells, both with the direct member's configuration---is within $0.5\%$ of the direct member at every horizon (paired-bootstrap fraction with lower RMSE 0.39--0.67), as the zero-origin diagnostic in Table~\ref{tab:shape} predicts. Tweedie and $\log(1+100y)$ targets are worse at every horizon (by 5--11\% and 3--7\%), and averaging five seeds of the direct member changes its RMSE by at most 0.61\%. A multi-output extremely-randomised-trees model \citep{geurts2006} of whole trajectories, as a third stacking member, changes no horizon beyond bootstrap noise (improvement fractions 0.34--0.55); a nearest-analogue model \citep{dellemonache2013} in a PCA space of history and weather signatures has 40.6/39.2/23.3/18.3\% higher RMSE than the direct member at $t{+}1/6/24/48$\,h and, as a third member, makes the stack worse at every horizon (improvement fractions 0.22--0.26). Within the tree family, every added member bought less than the bootstrap could resolve; as in the M5 findings \citep{makridakis2022} and in storm-outage work that found simpler models more accurate out of sample \citep{nateghi2014}, capacity mattered less than the target parameterisation. The sequence model is the one addition that survived the paired bootstrap, not by adding capacity on the same features but by reading the record differently.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} The code archive supplies the full pipeline, 34 tests, diagnostics, experiments, figures, validator, pinned requirements and commands. A fresh installation from the pinned requirements on Linux/CPython 3.11 reproduced the submitted predictions byte-for-byte (the stack alone also on CPython 3.13.5). The stack regenerates nested validation, Table~\ref{tab:results} and its predictions in about 200\,s on two CPU cores; the sequence model's cross-validation and final fit take about 70 minutes on one CPU thread, and because PyTorch arithmetic can differ in the last decimals across CPUs and BLAS builds the archive also writes the stack-only predictions. Manifests record input hashes, versions and runtime; identifier values, row order, NaN masks and cross-horizon consistency are validated. Windows execution is unverified.

\section{Limitations and conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
The evidence comes from one storm sequence in one region; the half-life and the hand-over point between restoration and damage are properties of this event and its utilities and should be re-estimated for another. Weather is reanalysis, not an operational forecast, so the scores are optimistic for real-time use, where forecast error at 24--96\,h lead would propagate. Folds are stratified but not spatially blocked, so mild optimism from correlated neighbours cannot be excluded, and although the stacking weights were fitted in nested folds, the choice among alternatives---including the sequence model's level target over a residual-from-prior variant, which scored similarly---was made on the same development data. The sequence model is also less transparent than the stack: we can measure what it adds but not attribute it to features, two seeds are few, and its cross-platform bit-reproducibility is weaker. The model produces point forecasts; for pre-positioning decisions a utility would want quantiles, which the same structure could provide with a pinball loss. The additions we would try first are the static county covariates the organisers list as optional enrichment---tree canopy, line miles, rural--urban class---which the outage literature finds informative for damage susceptibility \citep{cerrai2019,wanik2015}, and spatially blocked validation.

Our contribution combines one county trajectory from the fixed information set, a restoration prior for first-wave recovery, residual boosting for deviations, lead-dependent stacking as the balance shifts toward new damage, and a sequence-based reading of the same record averaged with the first. On held-out counties it removes 50\% of climatology RMSE at $t{+}1$\,h and 24\% at $t{+}48$\,h, with every component's contribution assessed by ablation.
